§1 Merlin Governance & Decision Model

Purpose
Show that Merlin operates inside deterministic governance and bounded authority — the core trust claim behind every other Merlin surface.
Major components
Authority chain (KEEP → Director → Acting Session → Merlin); capability classes (Observe, Analyze, Correlate, Recommend — live; Execute — future-only); Authorization Evaluation gate; two approval modes (Live Confirmation, Standing Pre-Authorization) plus the one named duress exception; the audit terminus.
Relationships
Authority flows one direction only and is never assumed by Merlin. The LLM is invoked only for Analyze/Correlate/Recommend — never for a standing-policy trigger. A refusal bypasses approval and execution entirely, going straight to audit. Every path, approved or refused, ends at the same audit terminus.
Why the site
Turns "advisor by default, executor only when authorized" from a claim into a checkable mechanism — the first thing a technically skeptical evaluator should see.
Partially resolved 2026-08-15 — narrowed, not a remaining "no exception" gap

Audit coverage: the rule was corrected, a smaller gap remains, honestly tracked

A direct code-inspection pass (2026-08-04, documentation/merlin-architecture-diagrams.html) found that of Merlin's three live surfaces, only the Incident Coordinator durably persisted both successful responses and ordinary refusals (into incident_log). Rather than requiring the other two surfaces to match that bar, a 2026-08-10 Director decision corrected merlin.md §8 itself: durable audit is now required only for actions that change the environment — Device Diagnosis and Onboarding Scan Analysis are analysis-only (never able to reach a mutating action, verified in merlin-rule-boundary.ts), so they were never actually required to carry that treatment. What remains, honestly tracked rather than closed by the rule change: their ordinary refusals still only reach console.warn, not a durable record — recorded as MERLIN-AUDIT-COVERAGE-PARTIAL in the project's roadmap. Standing-policy ambiguity events (§18) are, and always were, durably audited for all three routes (migration 068).

§2 Operational Data Path

Rebuilt 2026-09-17 — split out of the prior combined Hub/Spoke figure, which crammed this operational question together with the separate Control Plane/entitlement question (now §3) into one crowded picture and, in the process, ran two lines of Undercroft's own caption text directly on top of each other. Answers one question only: how does discovery/monitoring data actually move through a KEEP deployment?

Purpose
Show exactly how discovery/monitoring data moves through a KEEP deployment, and exactly where Undercroft sits relative to the KEEP application container — nothing about business/licensing relationships, which is a separate question answered in §3.
Major components
Client Devices and the Spoke (Customer Network); Local Devices and the Hub-Local Collector (the Hub's own network, present only for Solo or when a Hub also monitors its own site); the KEEP Application Container itself (one unified Docker image, Hub role); Undercroft (separately-provisioned Postgres, drawn outside the application container); bundled/supporting services.
Relationships
A Spoke and the Hub-Local Collector run identical discovery/poll code against their own respective networks — this is genuine symmetry, not "Hub manages, Spoke discovers." A Spoke's findings are tunneled to the Hub; the Hub-Local Collector's findings need no tunnel at all, since they already originate inside the same application container. Both paths converge on the one KEEP application container, which writes everything into Undercroft. Undercroft is never part of that container — it is separately provisioned, placed same-host or on separate infrastructure by MSP choice, with same-host placement creating a disclosed shared failure boundary. A Solo deployment is simply a Hub with zero Spokes, using only the Hub-Local Collector path.
Why the site
The single fact every visitor needs first: vendor-agnostic, one application container, and a clean, visually unambiguous separation between that container and its persistence layer.

§3 Control Plane, Entitlement & Heartbeats

New 2026-09-17 — split out of the prior combined Hub/Spoke figure specifically so this business/licensing question gets its own uncrowded space, separate from §2's operational data-path question. Answers: what business relationship exists between a deployment and KEEP's own Control Plane — and, just as importantly, what does not reach it.

Purpose
Establish the business/licensing relationship between a deployment and KEEP's own Control Plane, and state explicitly that operational/customer data never flows there — a question deliberately kept separate from §2's operational data-path question.
Major components
A simplified Hub and Spoke (full topology is §2); the external KEEPMSP.io Control Plane (business authority — licensing, entitlement, billing); the Hub's one-time registration plus ongoing heartbeat; the Spoke's own independent heartbeat; the license-cap-vs-ongoing-check distinction; the self-serve-tier-vs-staff-provisioned-Evaluation licensing paths.
Relationships
Both the Hub and the Spoke talk directly to the Control Plane — the Hub does a one-time registration plus an ongoing two-way heartbeat; the Spoke does its own direct two-way heartbeat, never relayed through the Hub. A Spoke's eligibility to register at all is capped by the Hub's own account-level license grant, but its ongoing heartbeat/entitlement status is then checked individually. The Control Plane structurally never stores customer operational data — devices, incidents, credentials, and scan results stay on the Hub's own Undercroft (§2), always.
Why the site
Answers a security-conscious evaluator's real question — "does my network data go anywhere near KEEP's own servers" — with an explicit, unmissable boundary statement, not an inference left to the reader.

§4 Merlin Architecture

KEEP → Merlin Governance → Merlin Narrative Layer → Future Merlin. Each layer sits strictly on top of the one below without replacing it. Verified against KEEP_PRODUCT_STRATEGY_V1.md §4 (layers 1–3, exact terms "KEEP," "Merlin Governance," "Merlin Narrative Layer") and merlin.md §13/§14/§17 (Future Merlin — a synthesis of three separate future-only sections, not a single named layer in the source).

Purpose
Establish what Merlin is — a layered extension of a complete, standalone platform, not an AI feature bolted onto a shell.
Major components
KEEP (deterministic platform), Merlin Governance (governance/audit/policy, free), Merlin Narrative Layer (optional AI layer, KEEP-managed default / BYOL), Future Merlin (cinematic install, behavioral risk research, multi-provider/offline).
Relationships
Each layer sits strictly on top of the one below without replacing it — KEEP functions with Core alone; Intelligence is removable; Future Merlin extends Core's governance rather than bypassing it.
Why the site
Directly supports the locked product-philosophy line "Merlin enhances KEEP, it isn't a separate product" — shows a prospect they're buying a complete deterministic platform first, AI second.

§5 Incident & Signal Intelligence

Purpose
Show KEEP's "evidence, not assertions" philosophy operating on ordinary day-to-day monitoring data.
Major components
Condition detection; Severity (color); Flash (urgency, condition-type driven); the two SLA categories (Operational Emergency vs. Security/Compliance); Compound Risk.
Relationships
Severity and flash are independent axes — one never implies the other. SLA category determines the response-time contract, signed per client. Two or more distinct conditions on one device escalate qualitatively, not by simple addition.
Why the site
The plainest demonstration of the product's daily value — a rules-driven, explainable signal model, not a black box.

§6 Root of Trust / Enrollment

Rebuilt 2026-09-16 from current source, not patched — see the finding below for exactly what this replaces. The 2026-07-08 "three independent trust decisions" model still names the right questions, but a real cryptographic identity/enrollment chain has since been built and, as of today, fully verified against live Production — this diagram now shows both, honestly distinguishing what's still unbuilt from what the old version simply never depicted. Two different things share the word "trust" below — do not conflate them: the first box names one narrow, specific, still-unbuilt claim (is this physical device genuinely KEEP-manufactured hardware, not a clone); everything below it is a real, separate, already-built cryptographic identity and enrollment chain that answers a different question entirely (which already-enrolled instance is this, and is its token/certificate genuine and unrevoked). KEEP is not without a trust mechanism today — it is specifically without device-manufacture attestation.

Purpose
Make KEEP's Spoke-enrollment security posture legible and checkable against what's actually built today, not what was built when this diagram was first drawn.
Major components
The three 2026-07-08 trust questions (KEEP Trust — still Future; Network Trust and Enrollment Authorization — Current); Persistent Logical KEEP-Instance Identity (Ed25519, generated pre-role-dispatch); Hub Signing-Key Provisioning (automatic, rotatable, revocable); the Ed25519-signed onboarding token (v2); Spoke mTLS client-certificate enrollment via CSR; bearer-token ingest auth as the universal, always-on gate; and the closed, live, end-to-end Production rehearsal.
Relationships
Network Trust and Enrollment Authorization are no longer just "one token issuance" — they now sit above a real, verifiable cryptographic chain: instance identity → signing-key provisioning → signed onboarding token → Spoke mTLS enrollment, alongside a bearer-token check that applies to every ingest/relay/enroll call regardless of certificate status. mTLS is additive hardening on two specific relay endpoints, never a replacement for the bearer-token gate. KEEP Trust remains the one independent question this chain does not answer.
Why the site
Answers "how do I know an impostor device can't join my network, and what's actually been proven, not just designed" — with the real mechanism and a real, dated, production closure record, while still naming the one honest gap rather than papering over it.

§7 Authentication & Sign-In

KEEP supports three deployment authentication methods — a KEEP-native local account, Active Directory/LDAP, and generic OIDC/SSO — plus exactly where multi-factor authentication does and doesn't apply. A fourth, non-customer-facing development fallback exists in the code and is shown here for completeness, clearly separated from the three real options.

Purpose
Make KEEP's own sign-in mechanics legible — which of the three supported methods a tech actually authenticates through, exactly where MFA does and doesn't apply, and where OIDC/SSO's pending-approval gate sits in that flow.
Major components
Local Account (bcrypt), Active Directory/LDAP (via ldapts), OIDC/SSO (a separate NextAuth provider) with its own pending/inactive gate for an unrecognized or not-yet-approved email; the MFA Gate hung only off the Local Account path. A development-only fallback also exists in the code (accepts any password when neither Local nor AD/LDAP applies) — drawn small, dashed, and visually separated from the three supported methods, never presented as a customer-facing deployment option.
Relationships
Local and LDAP are evaluated in that priority order for a given tech — the first that applies wins; the development-only fallback is what happens if neither does, shown here only because an existing finding on this page (below) depends on it being visible, not as a fourth peer choice. OIDC sits outside this chain entirely, reached only via its own sign-in button, and is the only path with its own denial branch (an unrecognized or inactive email is refused, not signed in). Every successful path converges on the same session; MFA is the only structural difference among the three supported methods, and it applies to Local accounts alone. Session establishment is authentication only — it says nothing about what that session can do. That's authorization, a separate mechanism covered in Figure 8.
Why the site
Answers a security-evaluating reader's actual question — "what happens when I sign in, and is MFA actually enforced" — with the real branching logic, not just a feature-list mention.

Backend selection is per tech, not per deployment

A Director can hand one contractor a local password inside an otherwise AD-integrated MSP — password_hash is checked per msp_techs row, not as a single deployment-wide switch. (Source: DECISIONS.md, "Auth Provider Abstraction.")

The installer doesn't offer Local as an install-time choice yet

chooser.js and install-pyx.sh's network-type question still only offers ldap, oidc, or skip — skip maps to the insecure development-only fallback shown above (dashed, not one of the three supported methods), meaning a real deployment can end up on it by installer choice, not just by leaving env vars unset in dev. Wiring Local into the installer scripts is separate, unbuilt work.

§8 Authorization: Roles & Permissions

What a signed-in session can actually do inside KEEP — a separate question from Figure 7's "how did this session get established," answered by an additive, union-based role model rather than a single rank.

Purpose
Make KEEP's authorization model legible once a session already exists — which roles a technician can hold, where those roles come from, and how they combine into what that technician can actually do.
Major components
Two independent role-assignment sources (AD/LDAP group mapping, manual assignment); three predefined roles (Technician, Senior Technician, Director); four composable roles (Remote-Access Specialist, Onboarding Coordinator, Compliance Reviewer, Security Administrator); the union operation that turns an assigned-role set into effective permissions.
Relationships
A technician's assigned roles are the combination of whatever AD/LDAP groups currently map to them and whatever a Director or Senior Technician assigned by hand — both can apply at once, independently, and neither source erases the other. Generic OIDC/SSO is deliberately absent from this pair: it authenticates identity only, and no OIDC group or claim currently maps to a KEEP role. Effective permissions are the union of everything every assigned role grants; there is no deny-list and no conflicting-role restriction in this version, so holding an additional role only ever adds access. The predefined and composable roles are drawn as equal, independent options specifically to avoid implying a ranked ladder is how the system decides access — permissions are the actual primitive.
Why the site
Answers the natural follow-on to Figure 7 — "OK, I'm signed in, so what can I actually do, and who decides that" — with the real model, not just "roles exist."

Live-verified end-to-end, not just designed

Confirmed against a disposable KEEP deployment, not only reviewed in source: role assignment through the real administrative screen, the permission union it produces, AD/LDAP-group-driven role mapping and revocation on re-sign-in, and coexistence between a manually assigned role and a directory-derived one all behaved exactly as described above.

§9 Tech Workbench & Remote Session Relay

Added 2026-09-16 — a real, fully-shipped, production-verified KEEP capability with no schematic representation anywhere before this. How a technician reaches a live RDP/SSH/VNC session on a client device without the Hub ever dialing into the client's network itself. Sourced from documentation/research/tech-workbench-v1-architecture.md (APPROVED 2026-08-08) and its implementation roadmap (Phase 6 CLOSED 2026-08-10, live-hardware-verified, 97/97 unit tests), cross-checked directly against the current route files.

Purpose
Show how a technician reaches a live remote session on a client device without the Hub ever gaining routed access into that client's network — a real, shipped capability this diagram library previously had zero representation of.
Major components
The Remote Session Broker (the one genuinely new Hub-side orchestration piece); the reused spoke_commands dispatch mechanism (one new command type, OPEN_RELAY_SESSION); the Spoke's own decoupled, short-interval relay poll; the new outbound Spoke Relay Channel; guacd-bridge's container-to-container bridge; the existing, unchanged Guacamole/guacd credential and protocol layer; and the new, append-only tech_workbench_session_audit table.
Relationships
The Spoke is the only component that ever dials into the client network, and only outbound — the same posture the Hub already holds for ordinary monitoring. Two of the Spoke↔Hub calls in this path (command pickup, relay-channel accept) are the same two endpoints Figure 6's mTLS layer hardens — this is what that hardening is for. No device credential is ever included in the relay/dispatch path; the tech's own saved credential is decrypted only once a real relay channel exists to hand it to. Merlin has no separate mechanism here at all — it shares the identical entry point a technician uses.
Why the site
A visitor asking "how does a technician actually reach a device at a client site, and does the Hub get routed access to do it" gets the real, live-verified answer — including the one governance fact (Merlin gets no shortcut) that matters most to a security-conscious evaluator.
